# Pre-release accuracy review — *What Would I Pay?*

**Reviewer:** nonpartisan fiscal-policy analyst (external accuracy sweep)
**Date:** 2026-09-09
**Scope:** `src/data/baseline2026.ts`, `src/data/spending.ts`, `src/data/platforms/*`, `src/data/states.ts`, `src/data/personas.ts`, `src/engine/calculate.ts`, `src/engine/attribution.ts`, and the research notes in `docs/research/03`, `04`, `08`.
**Method:** direct primary-source fetches (IRS Rev. Procs. 2025-32 and 2025-25, the enacted text of P.L. 119-21 from govinfo, CMS, HHS/ASPE, KFF, Tax Foundation, Treasury Final MTS, OMB Historical Table 1.4, National Priorities Project, Census P60-286, Sanders' financing paper), plus a 117-URL citation sweep. Roughly 130 fetches across this review and its delegated sub-tasks.

---

## Overall verdict

**This is unusually good work, and it is not ready to publish as-is.**

The numeric baseline is the strongest part of the project and it is close to flawless. I checked every parameter in `baseline2026.ts` against the governing primary source, and the tax-law core — all four 2026 bracket schedules including the deliberate $25 head-of-household offset, the capital-gains breakpoints, the standard deduction, the additional 65+ amounts, the CTC, all four EITC tiers with their phase-in/phase-out rates and the investment-income limit, the ACA applicable-percentage table, the 2025 and 2026 poverty guidelines for all three regions, the Medicare Part B premium, deductible and all five IRMAA tiers, the CMS age curve to three decimals, the KFF employer-premium figures, the Tax Foundation tariff cost, and the entire Treasury/OMB/NPP receipt chain — **confirmed exactly, without exception**. The SALT cap, senior deduction, and tips/overtime parameters, which the research notes flagged as not primary-sourced, I confirmed directly against the statutory text of P.L. 119-21, and they are right. Three open questions — the work-requirement caretaker-exemption age, the senior deduction's non-indexation, and whether Congress restored the enhanced ACA credits during 2026 (it did not) — are now resolved in the project's favor. Exactly one headline parameter remains unconfirmed, the Social Security wage base, and only because ssa.gov blocks automated access entirely. That is a better hit rate than most published calculators achieve.

The problems are almost entirely on the **platform** side, and they fall into three groups. First, a small number of genuine engine bugs, of which two matter: married-filing-separately households are given the tips, overtime and senior deductions that P.L. 119-21 expressly denies them, and Wisconsin is coded as a coverage-gap state when it is the one non-expansion state with no coverage gap. Second — and this is the serious one — **the modeling of individual platforms is not applied with symmetric rigor across the political spectrum.** Sanders' income-tax plan is modeled as a single top-bracket surtax while the 40/45/50% tiers below it and his capital-gains-as-ordinary-income provision are silently dropped; the Social Security Expansion Act's 16.2% tax on investment income — which I confirmed word-for-word in the enacted bill text of S.770, and which the research notes had wrongly suspected was a secondary-source error — is not modeled at all for Sanders, AOC or Booker; the mainstream Democratic and Harris NIIT increase is applied from $200k when the Greenbook applies it from $400k; Booker's offsetting rate increases are modeled at 39.6% when Penn Wharton scored them at 41/43%; Rubio gets his 2016 rate schedule *and* the 2026 standard deduction his plan would have abolished; Vance is modeled on a 2024 promise he has since abandoned; and the populist-right tariff rebate is modeled as fully funded by a revenue stream the app's own baseline says the Supreme Court eliminated. These errors do not all cut the same way — several favor Democrats, several favor Republicans — but they are large enough, and idiosyncratic enough, that a motivated critic on either side will find ammunition. The net pattern is worth naming: **the calculator is systematically gentler to the progressive platforms than their own sponsors' documents are.** Third, the citation layer has roughly a dozen bare-domain placeholders (`https://www.hawley.senate.gov/`, `https://kentuckylantern.com/`, `https://www.wkyt.com`, `https://gov.illinois.gov/`, `https://governor.maryland.gov/`, `https://mgaleg.maryland.gov`) that are not citations at all, three URLs that do not support the claim attached to them, one dead link, and two wrong-source attributions.

On **the field**, the set of 20 is defensible as a snapshot of who has a fiscal record worth modeling, but it is not a defensible snapshot of the 2028 race, and the app should stop implying it is. Two of its three horse-race labels look stale — Rubio overtook Vance in the Republican markets in June 2026 — Ossoff has denied running on the record more than once, and Tucker Carlson, who out-polls several included Republicans, is absent. The honest framing is "twenty figures whose tax and health platforms are on the record," not "the 2028 field." Separately, the field pass falsified two claims the app makes *about people* — that DeSantis has no federal fiscal position (he pitched a balanced-budget amendment in June 2026) and that Whitmer's tariff stance is unknown (she has opposed tariffs repeatedly and on the record). Claims about people carry a higher bar than modeling choices, and both should be corrected before launch.

**Recommendation: do not publish until the P0 and P1 items below are fixed.** The P0 list is about a week of work and mostly mechanical. The asymmetry items in P1 are the ones that determine whether this tool survives contact with a hostile reader.

## 3. Positions, confidence, lanes, and the field

### 3a. Structural findings that apply across the dataset

**The inheritance lanes are the single biggest fairness exposure, and the question in the brief is the right one.** Ossoff, Shapiro, Beshear, Moore, Newsom, Whitmer and Pritzker all inherit `party-dem`, whose parameter changes are drawn from the **Biden-Harris FY2025 Greenbook** — a 2024 document from an administration none of them served in. That means a Pennsylvania governor with no federal tax record is shown proposing a 39.6% top rate, an ARPA child credit, a childless-EITC expansion, capital gains as ordinary income above $1M, and a 5% NIIT, none of which he has ever endorsed. The app labels these "Party default" in the UI, which is the right instinct, but the *number at the top of the screen* is driven by them, and that number is what gets screenshotted and shared.

This is defensible as a modeling convention only if it is disclosed with the same prominence as the result. Two of the four named cases are worse than the others:

- **Ossoff** is handled best: the file uses `hold()` on CTC and payroll to block the party default where non-cosponsorship is documented. That is exactly the right mechanism. But he still inherits the Greenbook's top rate, capital-gains and NIIT changes despite the description calling him "the clearest moderate contrast in the Democratic field." The description and the model contradict each other.
- **Shapiro** has two positions, both cited to Wikipedia, one of which is a *state corporate* tax cut. Everything that moves his number is inherited. He should either get a real research pass or be presented with the inherited share of his result visually separated.
- **Beshear** is the sharpest case. His own record is a **state income-tax cut from 5% to 3.5%** — he is, on the app's own description, "the most fiscally moderate Democrat researched" — and the app then models him raising the federal top rate to 39.6% and taxing capital gains as ordinary income above $1M because he inherits the Greenbook. That is close to the opposite of his revealed fiscal preference.
- **Moore** is the most defensible of the four, because he has actually signed a tax increase on high earners, so the Greenbook inheritance points the same direction as his record.

**Vance and the enhanced ACA credits: the lane logic is correct.** I traced this specifically. `resolvedPositions` excludes an ancestor's position when a descendant has a position in the same area with an `apply` function, so `MAGA`'s `restoreEnhancedAca` never runs for Vance, and his own `apply` (setting `cliffAt400 = true`) leaves current law in place. **The output is right.** But the code comment — "Override the MAGA-lane restoration" — describes a mechanism that doesn't exist, and the `apply` is a no-op that would be clearer as `hold()`. More substantively: Vance is placed in the populist-right lane and then overrides its single most economically significant health position. If the lane's defining feature doesn't apply to its highest-profile member, the lane is doing less work than the taxonomy implies. Consider putting Vance in `party-gop` with the $5,000 CTC as his one populist departure.

**AOC's 70% and Sanders' 52% are treated inconsistently.** Both are historical figures (2019 and 2019/2020) with no current restatement. AOC's is rated `'low'` and the summary says "Never introduced as a bill; no 2025–26 restatement" — that is exemplary handling. Sanders' identical-vintage figure is rated `'medium'` with the parenthetical "not re-verified against a current document." Same evidentiary status, different confidence. Make them both `'low'`. Separately, note that the 52% *is* verifiable to a primary Sanders document (see W7/2b) — but that same document contains three more brackets the app doesn't model, so "verifying" it properly makes Sanders' plan considerably more expensive for upper-middle earners, not less.

**Rubio being modeled on his 2016 plan is disclosed but not sufficiently.** The description says "Treat as 'historical Rubio'" and every position carries a date. That is honest. The problem is W13 — the *combination* modeled (2016 rates + 2026 standard deduction) is not any plan Rubio ever proposed, and it is more generous than the real one. A user comparing Rubio to Vance is comparing a real platform to a favorable hybrid. Either model the $2,000 refundable personal credit and drop the standard deduction, or drop the bracket `apply` entirely and present Rubio as a GOP-baseline inheritor with historical notes.

**Harris's 2024 plan.** The description says plainly that "All figures are from the 2024 campaign, not a 2026 restatement," and the notes say "Needs a dedicated 2026 research pass." That is honest, and Harris is the one figure in the set with a genuinely complete off-the-shelf platform, so modeling it is reasonable. But it should not be presented alongside sitting officeholders' current positions without a visible vintage badge.

**Partisan framing in summaries and descriptions.** I read all 25 descriptions looking for asymmetric loading. The dataset is, on the whole, notably even-handed — it credits Hawley's Medicaid dissent, notes Newsom's Medi-Cal restrictions and Pritzker's immigrant-health cut against interest, calls the Democratic platform "silent on SALT" rather than papering over it, and describes the Libertarian platform's lack of numbers bluntly. Four places lean:

1. **`GOP.socialSecurityBenefits`**: "*Markets* the $6,000 senior deduction as 'no tax on Social Security'; benefit taxation itself was not repealed." Accurate, but "markets" is doing editorial work. "Describes… ; benefit taxation itself was not repealed" states the same fact without the sneer.
2. **`AOC.tipsOvertime`** quotes "a scam" and "a deal with the devil" in a position summary. Quoting is fine, but no Republican position summary carries comparable rhetorical quotation about a Democratic policy. Either quote symmetrically or paraphrase both.
3. **`DEM.description`** — "no increases under $400k" is repeated as fact; per W6 the model as implemented *does* increase taxes under $400k (the NIIT). Fix the model, or the claim.
4. **`LIBERTARIAN.role`** — "2026 platform (Jo Jorgensen exploratory committee, May 2026)" conflates a party platform with an individual's exploratory committee. The lp.org platform page shows a 2026 copyright, not a verified 2026 revision. Cite the platform's actual adoption date.

**Confidence ratings that are too high for inferred positions.** The `pos()`/`note()`/`hold()` distinction is good design, and the `'low'` ratings are used honestly. Three are over-rated:

- `LIBERTARIAN.ctc` and `LIBERTARIAN.eitc` at `'medium'` for positions the file itself marks "(inferred)". An inference from a platform that names neither program should be `'low'`.
- `VANCE.ctc` at `'medium'` for a $5,000 figure he floated once in August 2024 and has, per the app's own description, replaced with touting the enacted $2,200. This is the highest-leverage single position in the Republican half of the dataset — it changes a two-child household's result by $5,600 — and it models the abandoned promise rather than the current position. Either model $2,200 with the $5,000 as a note, or drop confidence to `'low'` and badge it "2024 campaign promise, not current position."
- `RUBIO.ctc` at `'high'` for "$2,500 per child" — the `'high'` is justified for the *historical* claim but the modeled parameter is a 2015 plan number.

**Two politicians have `hold()` used well and one has it missing.** Ossoff (CTC, payroll) and Gallego (payroll) correctly block party defaults on documented non-cosponsorship. **Whitmer, whose file explicitly says "her tariff stance could not be verified this session, so the party default applies," inherits the Democratic `targeted` tariff multiplier** — i.e. an unverified stance is being asserted through inheritance while the notes say not to attribute one. Use `hold()` or state the inheritance in the UI for that specific line.

## 4. Modeling fairness

I read `calculate.ts` and `attribution.ts` in full. The engine is clean, well factored, and the `attribute()` design — replaying positions in calculation order so deltas sum exactly — is a genuinely good idea that most calculators don't attempt. The `explainZero()` function, which tells a user *why* a position didn't affect them, is excellent and should be a selling point. The `AssumptionsPanel` exposing `employerPremiumToWages` and `employerPayrollPassthrough` as user toggles is the right architecture.

The fairness question is not whether the engine is biased — it isn't, structurally. It is whether the *choices* embedded in it lean, and several do.

| # | Choice | Which way it leans | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | **Single payer: 4% household premium charged, employer 7.5% payroll premium defaulted to zero passthrough, employer health premium also defaulted to not returning as wages.** | **Both directions, and the disclosure only mentions one.** The `PROGRESSIVE.notes` say "the employer payroll premium is shown only if you assume it passes through to wages; the calculator assumes it does not" — which reads as *flattering* single payer. But the same default also withholds the ~$20,143 employer family premium that would stop being paid. For a median employer-covered family, full passthrough is **+$20,143 in wages against a −$6,280 payroll premium — a net gain of ~$13,863.** The zero-zero default is therefore **unfavourable** to single payer for most employer-covered households, not favourable. | Rewrite the note to state both omissions and their net direction. Better: make the two toggles a single "assume full economic passthrough" switch, since assuming one without the other is not a coherent incidence model. |
| M2 | **Single-payer exemption modeled as a flat $29,000** rather than the filing-status standard deduction (W15). | Favours single payer for single filers (~$516/yr), slightly against it for joint filers. | Use `agi − standardDeduction[fs]`, per the source document. |
| M3 | **Tariff multipliers 0.25 / 0.5 / 1.0 / 1.25 for repeal / targeted / keep / expand.** | **These are pure judgment and the research notes admit it** ("these multipliers are the app's judgment, not sourced figures"). The asymmetry is that "repeal" retains 25% of the cost while "expand" adds only 25% — a repealer's benefit is capped at 75% of the baseline while an expander's harm is capped at 25%. That is a defensible reading of what is actually repealable (pre-2025 §301 tariffs survive any plausible repeal) but it systematically compresses the range on the pro-tariff side. | Surface the multiplier in the UI next to the tariff line, with the one-sentence rationale. Consider making it a user-adjustable assumption like the passthrough toggles. Also note the cap is applied **before** the multiplier, so "expand" can exceed the stated $3,000 ceiling (to $3,750). |
| M4 | **Tariff cost modeled as a flat 1% of gross income.** | Contradicts the app's own cited authority. `baseline2026.ts` cites Yale Budget Lab for the proposition that "tariffs are regressive as a share of income" and then implements a **proportional** schedule, which by construction is not regressive (until the $3,000 cap makes it regressive at the *top*). Understates the burden on low-income households; overstates it on the affluent. Also, `grossIncome` includes Social Security benefits and capital gains, so the retired persona is charged tariffs on portfolio income. | Either implement a declining-percentage schedule consistent with Yale's distributional table, or drop the Yale citation and describe the 1% as a flat approximation. And the funding source is, per the app's own baseline, extinguished. | At minimum, scale the rebate to surviving tariff revenue, or model it as $0 with the proposal shown as an unmodeled `note()`. If it stays, it needs the same "no financing modeled" caveat the Libertarian platform gets. |
| M6 | **Medicaid work requirement priced as `0.25 × $2,500 = $625/yr` expected cost.** | **Against Republicans, but defensibly so — with two caveats.** The 25% comes from CBO's ~5.3M-of-~20M, which is a national average over 2027–2034 including procedural disenrollment; applying it as a flat annual expected cost to every non-exempt adult is a strong reading. And the $2,500 uninsured cost is itself an unsourced assumption, so the whole line is assumption × assumption. | Keep it — expected-value framing is the honest way to model a probabilistic policy — but present it as "roughly a 1-in-4 chance of losing coverage" with the range, rather than a point estimate. The existing warning text already does this well; make sure the *number* carries the same hedge. Verify the 25% against CBO directly (cbo.gov 403'd this session). |
| M7 | **Libertarian modeled as tax removal only.** | **Strongly favours the Libertarian platform on the headline number** — zero federal income tax, zero payroll tax, zero tariffs, against the loss of ACA subsidies and Medicaid expansion. For most households this is by far the largest positive result in the app. | The `caveats.push()` disclosure is good and prominently worded. But a caveat string cannot compete with a large positive number. Recommend suppressing the headline figure for platforms flagged as structurally unfunded, or rendering it with a distinct "unfunded — no replacement modeled" treatment. **The same treatment should apply to Rand Paul** (business transfer tax not modeled), so the rule is applied by structure and not by party. |
| M8 | **No platform's financing is charged back to the household.** | **Symmetric in principle, opposite in effect.** Deficit-financed tax cuts (GOP/OBBBA) show as pure gains; deficit-financed coverage expansions (Dem/Progressive) show as pure gains.
